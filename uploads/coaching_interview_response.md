# AI Interview Response: Coaching Experience

---

Coaching has been a consistent and deliberate part of my leadership approach across more than 20 years, not a side responsibility. Over that time I've directly coached 40+ people into higher roles — from junior engineers into client-facing technical consultants, mid-level engineers into senior architects, and individual contributors into engagement leads. Let me give you the specifics.

**My core philosophy is "show by doing, then teach them to fish."** I don't delegate and disappear, and I don't lecture in the abstract. I bring people into real situations — client meetings, architecture debates, difficult scope conversations — demonstrate the approach first, then progressively transfer ownership, with structured recaps afterward so the lesson sticks beyond that one moment.

---

## Most Vivid Example: PwC — Distributed Global DSL Data Platform Team

At PwC I was chief architect and technical lead on a configuration-driven, event-driven data platform built for a global consumer markets client, subsequently productized and scaled across Azure, AWS, and GCP for multiple verticals. The team was 50+ people, distributed across onshore and offshore, including consulting firm architects, engineers, and client-side technical staff.

Two coaching stories stand out here.

The first was with a group of consulting firm architects. The conversation was about design philosophy — specifically, how to architect a unified, enterprise-deployable platform where core and use-case-based canonical data models and data products could be stored in a firm-wide repository, creating a competitive advantage through repeatable, vertical-specific outcomes across standardized ERP source systems. This was not just a technical debate; it had direct implications for how the firm would sell and market the platform. I coached these architects through the leap from "how do we build this for one client" to "how do we make this a scalable firm asset." That coaching translated directly into the marketing and sales materials that were used to bring the platform to market internally and externally.

The second was with a junior offshore engineer. The specific challenge was the design of the DSL configuration system governing how source data was ingested into the canonical data models — an architectural tradeoff between tactical implementation speed and strategic reusability of data quality rules and consumption patterns. Early on, this person could implement what they were told but couldn't yet explain the *why* to a client peer, let alone defend a design decision. Over multiple working sessions I walked through specific technical implementations and their downstream implications, starting concrete and moving toward pattern-level thinking. I showed them how to frame tradeoffs — short-term wins that don't box the client into a corner long-term — and how to guide a client decision-maker rather than just answer questions. Within a few months they were leading design conversations directly with their client-side peers. Critically, this wasn't a knowledge transfer session at the end of the engagement — it was ongoing, embedded operationalization. Both my team members and the client team learned how the platform worked, why it was designed that way, and how to extend it themselves. The proof: after the engagement ended, the client team launched a new use case independently, without requiring us to come back.

My recaps weren't formal — they were verbal 1:1s, Teams messages, and in-document comments that demanded polished, actionable outputs. If a meeting happened, we debriefed. If a document was weak, I didn't rewrite it — I added specific, pointed comments that required them to produce a better version. That discipline is how you build someone who can eventually own the room.

## Broader Pattern Across My Career

This same approach ran through every practice I've built. At NVISIA I created individualized growth plans for every person in the data practice — not a template, genuinely customized to where each person wanted to go. At SPR I tripled headcount and built a data and analytics practice from zero to $4.5M, which required not just hiring but actively developing engineers who had never operated on cloud-native platforms into consultants who could lead client engagements on Azure and AWS. At PSC Group I formalized a second tier of seniors specifically to create a coaching layer between me and junior engineers — so the coaching wasn't a bottleneck that ran only through me.

On the non-technical side, I've spent 15 years with MANATRA, working with over 100 high school and college NROTC students, teaching marine engineering in a hands-on context on an active training vessel. That's a different kind of coaching — building technical confidence in people who have no frame of reference yet — and it has sharpened my ability to meet people exactly where they are.

---

## Where It Hasn't Worked — and What I Learned

I'll be direct: not everyone makes the transition. I coached a mid-level engineer through a complex engagement — design and implementation of a data quality framework for a client's enterprise data governance program, where the goal was ensuring only trusted data reached data consumers. The technical work was solid, but this individual could not make the leap from individual contributor to servant-leader. Despite coaching sessions, peer teaching, live examples in both internal and client meetings of how to influence decisions through outcomes rather than authority, they were never able to fully internalize that model. They couldn't progress their career as a result. That experience reinforced something I believe strongly: coaching is an investment, but it requires the individual to meet you halfway. My job is to remove every obstacle I can and create every opportunity I can — but I can't want it more than they do. Recognizing that limit early — and being honest with the person about it — is itself a coaching skill.

---

## Summary

Coaching isn't something I do in addition to delivery — it's embedded in how I deliver. The measure I'm most proud of isn't the 40+ people across my career. It's that on the engagements where I've led, the client teams didn't just receive a platform — they left capable of owning and extending it themselves.
